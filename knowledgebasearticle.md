# How to structure a knowledge base article
A consistent structure helps readers find answers quickly and helps writers publish content that's easy to scan, review, and maintain. This article describes the sections of a knowledge base article and explains what to include in each one.

## Overview
The overview tells readers what the article is about and whether it's relevant to them. Readers decide within seconds whether to keep reading, so keep this section short.

In your overview, include:
- **Who or what the article covers**. Name the organization, team, product, or feature at the center of the article.
- **The main topic**. State the subject in one sentence, for example, "Contoso is modernizing its policy management with Power Apps."
- **Context for the reader**. Add one or two sentences of background, such as the organization's size, industry, or scope.

## Challenges
The Challenges section explains why a change was needed. It gives readers the context to understand the solution and helps them recognize similar problems in their own work.

In this section, include:
- **The starting point**. Describe the previous system, process, or situation.
- **The problem**. Explain what wasn't working, such as limitations, costs, or gaps in functionality.
- **The impact**. Describe how the problem affected users, teams, or the business.
- **The trigger**. Mention the event or decision that started the change, if there was one, such as a merger, a new business goal, or a product end of life.

Write about the problem from the reader's perspective. Use plain language and avoid blaming people or tools. If the change required trade-offs, say so. For example, explain if certain processes couldn't be replicated exactly in the new system.

## Solution

The Solution section explains what was implemented and how it addresses each challenge. Think of it as the high-level answer. The details come next, in the Components section.

In this section, include:
- **The approach**. Summarize what you did and why you chose it.
- **The products and technologies**. Name them accurately and use the official product names on first mention.
- **How the solution maps to the challenges**. Make the connection explicit, so readers can see that each problem has a corresponding answer.
- **The benefits**. Describe results such as saved time, reduced costs, improved adoption, or better collaboration. Use measurable data when you have it, and cite the source or timeframe.

Keep this section at a conceptual level. Use present tense and active voice. For example, write "The app stores policy data in Dataverse" instead of "Policy data is stored in Dataverse by the app."

## Components
The Components section breaks the solution into its parts. Readers use this section to understand how the pieces fit together and what each one does.

Start with a short introduction that lists the components. Then describe each component in its own subsection.

For each component, include:
- **A descriptive subsection heading**. Use sentence-style capitalization, for example, "Canvas app user experience."
- **What the component does**. Explain its purpose in one or two sentences.
- **Who uses it**. Identify the audience, such as employees, administrators, or developers.
- **Key features**. Describe the capabilities that matter most to the reader.
- **A visual**. Add a diagram or screenshot when it clarifies the explanation.
- **Governance, security, or limitations**. If these topics apply, cover them as a subsection or as part of the relevant component.

### Add visuals
When you add a diagram or screenshot:
1. Introduce it with a lead-in sentence, such as "The following image shows the home page, where users can browse and search policies."
1. Write alt text that describes what the image shows and why it matters. Don't start with "Image of" or "Screenshot of" followed by a filename.
1. Make sure the image is legible at the size it's displayed, and remove sensitive or personal data.
1. Don't rely on the image alone to convey information. Always explain the key points in text.
